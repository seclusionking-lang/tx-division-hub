Leadership Directive

From: Douglas Gormley, Director & JF, Associate Director — Effective immediately

I. The Standard We Expect

You are a manager, not a participant.

Your team's output is your output. When a metric slips, the question is not "why didn't they perform?" — it is "why didn't you catch it, address it, and correct it?" Leadership is not a title. It is a daily discipline of observation, communication, and accountability — especially in a remote environment where visibility requires effort and intentionality.

Remote does not mean invisible.

Managing a remote team demands more structure, not less. Your team takes cues from you. If you are inconsistent, unavailable, or vague about expectations, your team will mirror that ambiguity directly into their work product. You are the connection point between the division's goals and the daily behavior of your team. Own that role completely.

Metrics are your scoreboard — not mine.

Every department has measurables. If you do not know your team's current performance on every metric as of today, that is the first problem to solve. You cannot manage what you do not measure, and you cannot improve what you do not track.

II. Non-Negotiable Behaviors

Daily check-ins are mandatory.

Every working day. What was completed yesterday, what is the priority today, where are the blockers. Document outcomes. Not optional based on workload.

Weekly metric reviews are required.

Review performance metrics with your team every week. Gaps identified, causes diagnosed, corrective actions assigned with names and deadlines. Report summary to JF and the Director weekly.

Underperformance is addressed within 48 hours.

Documented one-on-one within 48 hours of any missed metric or standard. What happened, what the expectation is, what changes are needed, follow-up date. Ignoring underperformance is a management failure.

You will develop your people, not just direct them.

One developmental goal per team member per quarter. Coach toward it actively. You are building a division — not just running a queue.

III. Department-Specific Accountability

EM — Order Intake

Order intake accuracy and TaT are your primary metrics. Track error rates and throughput per team member weekly. Intake is the foundation of the entire division pipeline. Accepted TaT standard is 3–4 days. Division is currently hitting 4–5 days — consistently at or above the upper bound. Immediate goal is reliable delivery within 3–4 days before tightening toward 3.

MG — Orders Logistics

Order flow, routing accuracy, and logistics turnaround are your metrics. You are the operational bridge between intake and fulfillment. Maintain a live view of order status daily. Flag at-risk items before they escalate.

JM — Case Managers / Expediting

Client-facing responsiveness and case progression velocity are your metrics. Your team is the voice of this division to law firms. Response time, follow-up cadence, and case closure rates are tracked. Every expedite request is a signal — read them proactively.

VA — Records Intake

Records receipt, QC, and 24-hour processing TaT are your primary metrics. Every record that enters your department must be checked for completeness and legal compliance before it moves downstream. Incomplete records returned to Expediting represent rework that costs the entire division time. You own the quality gate. Nothing passes through your department that has not been verified.

GG — Billing & Production

Billing accuracy, invoice cycle time, and production output are your metrics. Reconcile daily, identify error patterns weekly, maintain a clean production log. Zero-defect ambition. As team lead, model the accountability standards in this directive every day.

IV. The Leadership Compact

We will invest in you — and we expect performance in return.

JF and I are committed to your development. You will receive coaching, frameworks, feedback, and resources. In return, show up every day as a leader. Ask hard questions. Surface problems early. Hold your team with respect and firmness. The only path forward is through accountability, clarity, and consistent execution.

Root Cause Assessment

Primary: Absence of a defined intake workflow

When distribution is ad hoc, the system only works at the speed and initiative of its most motivated person on any given day. Volume spikes expose this immediately — there is no structured mechanism to absorb demand. The team is not necessarily underperforming; the system is not built to perform.

Secondary: No real-time queue visibility for EM

A manager who cannot see the queue cannot manage it. EM's reliance on a manual spreadsheet means decisions are reactive rather than proactive. By the time EM knows there is a problem, TaT has already slipped.

Order type complexity is an untracked variable

Subpoenas, authorizations, and DWQs carry different data entry complexity and time requirements. If all three are processed with no weighting or specialization, high-complexity types silently inflate TaT without anyone understanding why.

Mangalam dependency is an unmanaged risk

Mangalam processes standard order data entry overnight and prepares notice packets. Every morning the team performs QC on Mangalam's output before the day's real work begins. If Mangalam's error rate is high, QC absorbs significant morning capacity — silently inflating TaT before a single new order is touched. EM must track Mangalam error rates daily and escalate to JF if overnight output quality degrades.

Department scope is broader than intake alone

Order Intake does not just onboard orders. The department also runs a three-part location verification process, prepares complex paperwork manually, files documents with the court, distributes notice copies to all counsel, and sends order confirmations to clients. Rush orders are handled entirely in-house same day. This full scope must be factored into capacity planning — TaT is not just data entry time.

In a physical office, a manager's personality gaps are partially offset by proximity — people can see effort, energy, and intention. In a remote environment, only output and communication exist. An I-style manager who avoids hard conversations cannot hide behind enthusiasm in a remote setting — the silence shows up in missed metrics. A C-style manager who over-processes every decision creates bottlenecks the team cannot see around. DISC tells JB exactly where each manager's wiring will create friction under the remote conditions this division operates in.

DISC maps directly to the five quiz domains

Accountability gaps are predictable by type. D-styles over-hold — they push accountability so hard it becomes intimidation. S-styles under-hold — conflict avoidance protects relationships at the expense of standards. I-styles are inconsistent — great in good times, avoidant when performance drops. C-styles hold the standard but struggle to deliver the feedback humanely. Knowing the type tells JB which coaching intervention will land and which will be resisted.

Facts vs. Emotions Assessment

Array Records Retrieval — Texas Division  ·  Required for all managers before any PIP, CAP, or Termination escalation

Why this matters. HR requires documented facts — not manager impressions — to authorize a PIP, CAP, or Termination. A report built on emotion or assumption will be rejected, will expose the division to legal risk, and will undermine your credibility as a manager. The standard is simple: if you cannot point to a date, a number, or a documented event, it does not belong in a performance report.
The 68/32 Problem

What we are seeing in current manager reporting

Current performance updates from the management team are running approximately 68% factual content and 32% emotional or conjectural content. That 32% is not just unhelpful — it is actively harmful. It dilutes the factual record, gives HR grounds to question the entire report, and in a legal challenge can be used to argue that the termination or disciplinary action was driven by personal bias rather than documented performance failure. Every percentage point of emotion in a performance report is a percentage point of legal exposure.

The Four Questions Every Manager Must Answer Before Escalating
1
What is the specific metric or standard that was not met?

Name it exactly. "68 files per day." "90-minute client response." "98% accuracy." "3-day mail-out TaT." If you cannot name the specific standard, you do not yet have a documentable performance issue — you have a feeling.

2
On what specific dates did the failure occur?

List the dates. Every one of them. If you cannot list dates, you have not been tracking — and if you have not been tracking, the failure is partly a management failure, not solely a performance failure. HR will ask this question first.

3
What documented coaching or correction has already occurred?

Date of conversation, what was said, what was agreed to, what the follow-up date was. If the coaching was not documented at the time, document it now from memory with a note that it is a recollection. An undocumented coaching conversation is a conversation that, legally, may not have happened.

4
What happened after the coaching?

Did performance improve? For how long? Did it revert? Are you escalating because coaching has failed — or because you have not yet coaching? HR and JF will want to see the coaching arc before authorizing any formal action.

The PIP / CAP / Termination Evidence Standard

PIP — Personal Improvement Plan

Required evidence: specific metric(s) not being met, documented dates of underperformance, at least one documented coaching conversation that preceded the PIP, clear statement of the standard the employee must meet, timeline for improvement, and consequences if not met. Emotion-free. Date-stamped. Metric-referenced.

CAP — Corrective Action Plan

Required evidence: all PIP documentation plus evidence that the PIP standard was not met within the defined timeline, dates of continued underperformance after PIP, any additional coaching documented during the PIP period. The CAP is the escalation from improvement to formal correction. Every element must be traceable to a date and a number.

Termination

Required evidence: full PIP and CAP documentation trail, evidence of continued failure to meet the CAP standard, confirmation that the employee was informed of consequences at each stage, and a clean factual summary that contains zero emotional language. HR will read this as if a judge might read it — because in some cases, one will. If your documentation would not hold up in an employment hearing, it is not ready.

Bottom line for every manager. If you are coming to JF with a performance concern about a team member, come with dates, numbers, and documented conversations — not feelings, observations, or what other team members have said. JF will send you back to gather the facts if you do not have them. DG will not authorize a PIP, CAP, or Termination without a clean factual record. Build the habit of documenting in real time — not when you decide you have a problem.

Framework: Extreme Ownership — Jocko Willink
Former Navy SEAL commander, combat leader, and author of Extreme Ownership and The Dichotomy of Leadership. Core teaching: there are no bad teams, only bad leaders. The leader owns everything — every outcome, every failure, every gap.
Core principles and application to this division

Extreme Ownership

There are no excuses. If the TaT is running 4–5 days instead of 3, EM owns that — not the volume, not the team, not the system. The moment a manager begins explaining why something went wrong instead of owning that it went wrong and fixing it, they have exited leadership and entered victimhood. Coach every manager to lead every debrief with: "Here is what I owned. Here is what I am changing."

Cover and Move

Departments in this division are not competing units — they are a single pipeline. When Order Intake slips, Orders Logistics absorbs the impact. When Logistics stalls, Case Managers feel it. Cover and Move means EM, MG, JM, and GG must communicate laterally and support each other's success, not operate in silos. JB should coach cross-departmental awareness as a leadership responsibility.

Simple

If a manager cannot explain their department's workflow in plain language to someone new, the workflow is too complex — or the manager does not understand it well enough. Jocko's principle: simplify everything until it is executable under pressure. The queue assignment system EM is building must be simple enough that any rep can follow it on their worst day.

Prioritize and Execute

When a spike day hits, managers cannot solve five problems at once. They must identify the highest-priority problem, solve it, then move to the next. Coach managers to resist the panic of simultaneous crises and instead triage clearly: what breaks the client relationship if unaddressed in the next hour? Start there.

Decentralized Command

DG and JF cannot be the decision-makers for every operational problem. Managers must be empowered — and trained — to make decisions within their lane without escalating everything upward. JB should coach each manager to define their decision authority: what can they decide alone, what requires JF, and what requires DG. Clarity here reduces bottlenecks and builds confidence.

The Dichotomy

Jocko's second framework: every leadership virtue taken too far becomes a vice. A manager too focused on accountability becomes a micromanager. Too empathetic becomes an enabler. Too decisive becomes reckless. JB should help each leader find their personal dichotomy edge — where their strength tips into weakness — and build the self-awareness to self-correct in real time.

Framework: Start With Why — Simon Sinek
Author of Start With Why, Leaders Eat Last, and The Infinite Game. Core teaching: people do not follow what you do — they follow why you do it. Leaders who lead with purpose build teams that perform beyond compliance.
Core principles and application to this division

Start With Why

Every manager in this division can tell their team what to do. Very few can tell them why it matters. The why for this division is real and meaningful: law firms depend on these records to represent clients who may be injured, wronged, or fighting for their rights. When a record is delayed, a case can stall, a settlement can be held up, a person's justice is delayed. EM's team is not entering data — they are moving cases forward. JB should help every manager articulate this purpose to their teams in their own words.

Leaders Eat Last

Sinek's research on military and organizational trust: teams perform best when they feel safe — when they know their leader will protect them, advocate for them, and not sacrifice them to look good upstairs. For remote managers, this is especially critical because the team cannot see the leader's behavior. Managers must demonstrate that they go to bat for their team — with DG, with JF, with clients — and that they absorb pressure rather than pass it down.

The Circle of Safety

When people feel safe inside their team, they direct their energy outward toward performance rather than inward toward self-protection. A team that fears their manager hides problems. A team that trusts their manager surfaces them early. JB should assess each manager: do their team members bring problems to them, or do they hide them? The answer reveals the safety level of the team environment.

The Infinite Game

This division is not running a sprint — it is building a sustainable operation. Sinek's infinite game principle: leaders with a finite mindset optimize for the next metric. Leaders with an infinite mindset build the conditions for sustained excellence. JB should help DG and JF think beyond the 60-day TaT fix and toward the question: what kind of division do we want to be known for in three years, and are today's behaviors building toward that?

Trust and Performance

Sinek's data is clear: trust precedes performance, not the other way around. Managers who lead with fear get short-term compliance. Managers who lead with purpose and safety get long-term discretionary effort — people who do more than is required because they want to, not because they are told to. For a remote team especially, discretionary effort is the only kind of effort a manager cannot force. It must be earned.

Framework: First Principles & Operations as a Manufacturing Process — Elon Musk
Musk's operational philosophy: question every assumption, reduce everything to its fundamental truths, then rebuild from the ground up. Applied to operations: every process is a manufacturing line. Every step must earn its place. Waste, delay, and complexity are enemies of performance.
Core principles and application to this division

First Principles thinking

Most people reason by analogy — "this is how we've always done it." First Principles reasoning asks: what is actually true here, and what can we build from those truths? Applied to Order Intake: the question is not "how do we improve our current intake process?" It is "what does a perfect intake process look like if we designed it from scratch, knowing only the inputs and the required output?" EM should be coached to design her queue system from first principles — not to patch what exists.

Operations as a manufacturing process

Musk treats every operation — whether rockets or cars or records retrieval — as a manufacturing line. Every order that enters the Texas Division is a unit moving through a production system: Intake → Logistics → Case Management → Billing → Output. Every step that adds delay without adding value is waste. JB should help managers see their department not as a team doing work but as a station in a production line — where throughput, defect rate, and cycle time are the fundamental metrics.

The 5-Step Algorithm

Musk's operational framework for any process: (1) Make the requirement less dumb — question why the requirement exists at all. (2) Delete the part or process — if you are not adding back at least 10% of what you deleted, you did not delete enough. (3) Simplify and optimize — but only after deleting. (4) Accelerate cycle time — but only after simplifying. (5) Automate — but only after all prior steps. Applied here: before EM builds a queue system, ask whether every current intake step is necessary. Delete first. Then simplify. Then optimize. Then accelerate.

The Idiot Index

Musk's metric for process waste: if a step costs significantly more time or money than its raw components justify, the "idiot index" is high. Applied to TaT: if an order takes 4–5 days when the actual processing time per location is 8–30 minutes, the gap between actual work time and elapsed time is the idiot index. That gap is filled with waiting, queue ambiguity, handoff friction, and invisible bottlenecks. JB should challenge managers to identify their idiot index — what is the gap between the time work is actually happening and the total elapsed time? That gap is the coaching target.

Move fast, fix fast

Musk's production philosophy: a slow feedback loop is a broken system. In manufacturing, a defect detected at the end of the line costs ten times more to fix than one caught at the source. In records retrieval, an intake error that reaches Case Management costs significantly more rework than one caught by EM's team before the order leaves the department. JB should coach every manager to push quality control upstream — catch it at your station, not someone else's.

Rejection of bureaucracy

Musk is famous for eliminating meetings, approvals, and process steps that exist by inertia rather than necessity. For managers recalibrating to a higher standard, the temptation is to add process — to make things feel more controlled. JB should coach against this instinct: every new step, report, or check-in must justify its existence. If it does not directly improve output quality, speed, or visibility, it is overhead. Build lean, then add structure only when absence of structure creates a proven problem.

Synthesis — applying all four frameworks together

The integrated leadership model for this division

These four frameworks are not competing — they are complementary layers of the same leadership standard:

FrameworkWhat it developsApplied to this division
Jocko WillinkAccountability and ownershipEvery manager owns every outcome. No excuses. Debrief everything.
Simon SinekPurpose and trustTeams follow leaders who create safety and lead with why. Remote teams especially.
Biblical principlesCharacter and integrityStewardship, servant leadership, and truth-telling as the non-negotiable foundation of sustainable leadership.
Elon Musk / First PrinciplesOperational excellenceEvery process is a manufacturing line. Eliminate waste. Measure everything. Rebuild from truth, not tradition.

A manager who owns outcomes (Jocko), leads with purpose (Sinek), operates with integrity (Biblical), and thinks in first principles (Musk) is a manager who will build something that lasts. JB's job is to develop all four dimensions simultaneously — because a leader strong in one and weak in another will eventually fail at the point of their weakness.
